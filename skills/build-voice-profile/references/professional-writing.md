# Professional writing interview

Use these optional modules when customer or investor writing matters to the person. Choose relevant gaps; do not administer every prompt or turn the twelve examples into a quota. Ask one concrete question at a time and follow the actual answer. Keep personal and everyday samples already gathered.

## Establish only the missing context

Reuse what is already known about the recipient, relationship, communication channel and intended outcome. If essential context is missing, ask a brief question such as "Who is this going to, and what would you like them to do after reading it?" Then move into a writing sample.

Invite a real situation or an existing message, with names and confidential details omitted where useful. Each imagined scenario below is hypothetical; say so when using it and let the person substitute a real example. Adapt the scenario to their work without inventing facts about their business. Do not suggest the wording, opinion or commercial position they should adopt.

Ask for the words they would actually send. If they first explain their private reaction or strategy, retain that context separately and invite the sendable version. A sample can be brief and conversational; professional does not mean more formal by default.

## Customer module

1. **Explain value.** Imagine a prospective customer asks what your offering would help them do. What would you write back? Use their stated problem if one is already known.
2. **Discover and qualify.** Imagine a prospect says they are interested but gives little context. What would you ask them next in a message? Let the answer reveal how they explore need, fit, decision process or commercial constraints; do not supply a checklist of questions for them to copy.
3. **Follow up with an ask.** Imagine a customer conversation has gone quiet after a useful meeting. What follow-up would you send? Use the actual agreed next step if supplied, and observe how familiarity changes the request.
4. **Handle an objection or disagreement.** Imagine a prospect says they do not see enough reason to change their current approach. How would you reply? If the answer stays abstract, invite one concrete objection they have actually encountered and their response.
5. **Set a commercial boundary.** Imagine a customer requests extra work outside the agreed scope without changing the price. What would you send them? Follow their actual position on pricing, flexibility and trade-offs rather than assuming they should refuse or discount.
6. **Give an honest difficult update.** Imagine a delivery is delayed and the revised completion date is still uncertain. What update would you send the customer? Observe how they explain responsibility, impact and next steps without nudging them towards a promise.
7. **Build trust with evidence.** Imagine a customer asks for proof of a result you cannot yet substantiate. What would you write back? If a real example is used, distinguish available evidence, limitations and any proposed verification.

Where a meaningful relationship contrast is missing, reuse one sampled situation with a long-standing customer or a first-time contact, whichever has not been sampled. Ask for that version separately, not as an extra task inside every question.

## VC and investor module

1. **Introduce yourself and the business.** Imagine you are contacting an investor who does not know you. What short introduction would you send to open a conversation? Use only the person's supplied description of the business and reason for contacting that recipient.
2. **Follow up after a meeting.** Imagine you have had a useful first investor meeting. What follow-up would you send? Use actual discussion points and agreed actions where available; do not invent interest or commitments from the investor.
3. **Report progress candidly.** Using a real period they can discuss, ask: "What brief investor update would you send about the progress and difficulties in that period?" If no real period is available, invite them to supply a hypothetical situation before drafting. Observe how they select traction evidence and explain uncertainty or setbacks.
4. **Express ambition under challenge.** Imagine an investor questions whether the business can become large enough. How would you reply? Let the person supply their ambition, reasoning and evidence; do not turn aspiration into a forecast or make up market figures.
5. **Discuss funding.** Imagine an investor asks why you are raising now and what the funding would enable. What would you write back? Use only funding plans and figures the person supplies; they can explain what is undecided or decline to share numbers.

Distinguish introductions to unfamiliar investors from updates to existing backers. Do not infer a preference for hype, jargon, brevity or aggressive certainty simply because the recipient is a VC.

## Calibrate on short messages

Once there is enough relevant evidence, show a short draft for a situation the person actually needs, or an explicitly illustrative scenario they have supplied. When both audiences matter, try a customer message and an investor message in separate turns. Use meaningful audience contrasts rather than merely swapping the recipient's name.

Aim for a usable, sendable sample based on supplied facts. Clarify material missing facts before presenting a draft as ready to send. If the person prefers an incomplete example, mark placeholders and label it a draft. Never invent traction, performance, prices, dates, funding figures, commitments or assurances. Keep current facts, proposed actions, aspirations and uncertain estimates distinct.

After showing the draft, ask one focused feedback question. Choose the dimension that needs testing, and cover others in later turns only where useful:

- **Tone:** "Which phrase feels too formal or too familiar for this recipient?"
- **Specificity:** "Where would you be more concrete, or leave something out?"
- **The ask:** "Is that the next step you would actually ask for, in words you would use?"
- **Certainty:** "Which claim sounds more definite than you would make it?"

Treat the person's replacement wording as stronger evidence than the first draft. Revise the affected passage and profile. Approval of one message supports that example; it does not validate every professional register. Showing a sendable draft does not authorise sending it.

## Add the supported findings to the profile

Keep the professional extension concise and connected to the existing voice profile. Record observed customer-versus-investor differences in explanation, warmth, commercial directness, evidence, ambition and asks only when the samples support them. Where there is no demonstrated difference, say so rather than inventing one.

Separate explicit preferences, observed patterns with short anchors, draft corrections and remaining gaps. Note which audience, relationship and situation each sample covers, including differences between private intent and sendable wording. A pricing response does not establish the person's investor-update style, and one confident introduction does not show how they handle bad news.

Retain only the personal detail needed to guide future writing. Samples show language choices; they are not permission to reuse confidential business facts or treat past figures and commitments as current. Keep personal answers and profiles separate from the reusable interview method.
