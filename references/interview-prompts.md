# Interview prompts

These are optional starting points, not a script to read all at once. Ask one at a time, adapt the context and avoid inventing a real incident involving a named person. State hypothetical situations as hypothetical.

## A flexible fifteen-question route

1. What's something you did recently outside work that you really enjoyed?
2. What's a small thing that can make an ordinary day better?
3. What's something that reliably irritates you more than it probably should?
4. Imagine a friend wants to try a hobby or activity you know well. What would you tell them before they start?
5. What's a childhood memory you still enjoy telling people about?
6. What book, show, game or place do you keep coming back to, and what brings you back?
7. Imagine someone close to you has had a rough day. What would you text them?
8. Imagine a friend invites you to something you don't want to do. How would you reply?
9. When did you last change your mind about something?
10. Someone has done a piece of work or helped you in a way you really appreciate. What would you say to them?
11. Imagine a colleague proposes making a costly service free before anyone has worked out the cost. How would you respond?
12. Imagine someone asks for an update on something you haven't started yet. What would you tell them?
13. What does an ordinary weekend morning look like for you at the moment?
14. What's something you're quietly proud of that doesn't come up much?
15. What's something you're hoping will happen over the next few months?

If work is not relevant, replace the colleague scenarios with familiar situations: choosing an expensive group trip, asking someone to return a borrowed item, or explaining why a household plan will not work. Avoid assuming that the person has children, a partner, particular abilities or a specific career.

## Useful follow-ups

- For a story: "What happened next?" or "What made that bit memorable?"
- For a very short answer: follow it only if curiosity or a missing distinction warrants it. Otherwise continue.
- For a vague opinion: "Can you give me an example?"
- For internal frustration: "If you were actually sending a message and wanted them to stay engaged, what would you write?"
- For a missing disagreement sample: offer a specific, plausible bad suggestion in a familiar domain. Do not ask the person to invent the whole scenario.
- For an unclear transcript: clarify the meaning if it affects the profile. Do not confidently label a transcription error as a signature expression.

## Calibration prompts

Choose a small number of situations supported by the interview, such as a practical request, a personal text and a brief explanation. Use the person's own material as the basis. Show the writing before asking for feedback.

Ask a focused question, for example:

- "Which bit would you never say?"
- "Is that request too soft, too sharp or about right?"
- "How would you say the first sentence yourself?"
- "Does that sound too polished for a message to this person?"

Do not ask all four at once. If the person says it feels right, record that as support and move on; do not keep demanding criticism.

## Evidence to retain

Keep a small set of representative excerpts with context and the writing implication. Separate actual words from lightly cleaned quotations and assistant-generated drafts. Record direct corrections and unresolved gaps. Do not preserve an entire personal interview merely because it is available.
